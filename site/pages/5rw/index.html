4 Signs Your Dog's Gut May Be Out of Balance—and the 30-Day Routine I Recommend

Grass eating. Loose stools. Paw licking. A tummy you can hear from across the room. These everyday clues don't all mean the same thing—but together, they can be a good reason to look more closely at your dog's digestive health.

By Dr. Levi Cohen, DVMUS veterinarian · Vet Consultant to Ruffora

One of the most useful things a dog parent can do is notice patterns. A single strange stool or one afternoon of grass eating is rarely the whole story. What matters is what keeps happening, what happens together, and whether your dog otherwise seems comfortable and well.

The gut is home to a large community of microorganisms. That microbiome helps break down food, produce useful compounds and communicate with the immune system. Diet changes, stress, medication, illness and other factors can disturb that environment. The signs are often ordinary enough to dismiss—until they become a pattern.

A quick veterinary noteNone of these signs diagnoses a “gut imbalance” on its own. Allergies, infection, parasites and other medical conditions can look similar. Sudden, severe or persistent symptoms should be evaluated by your veterinarian.
Sign 1

Your dog keeps eating grass

Dogs eat grass for many reasons: curiosity, habit, boredom, dietary preference, or simply because they like it. But when grass eating suddenly becomes frequent—especially alongside gurgling, gas, appetite changes or unusual stools—I pay attention to the digestive context.

Keep a simple note on your phone: when it happens, what your dog ate beforehand, and whether any other signs appeared that day. Patterns are far more useful to your veterinarian than a single isolated moment.

Sign 2

Loose stools, gas, or a gurgly tummy keep returning

A dog's stool naturally varies, but repeated softness, urgent bathroom trips, excess gas or loud intestinal sounds deserve a closer look. Food changes, rich treats, stress, parasites and health conditions are all possible contributors.

For an otherwise healthy dog with occasional digestive variability, consistency in diet and routine is often the best place to start. Change one thing at a time, give it time, and track what happens.

Sign 3

Paw licking or itching seems to follow the tummy trouble

The gut and immune system are closely connected, which is why skin and digestive signs are sometimes discussed together. That does not mean every itchy dog has a gut problem. Environmental allergies, food reactions, fleas, yeast, bacterial infection and irritation can all cause licking or scratching.

If the skin is red, broken, painful, smelly or getting worse, see your veterinarian. Gut support can complement a sensible wellness routine; it should not replace diagnosis or treatment.

Sign 4

A “corn chip” or yeasty smell keeps coming back

A mild odor can be normal. A strong or recurring smell from paws, ears or skin—especially with head shaking, discharge, redness or discomfort—can point to yeast or bacterial overgrowth and deserves veterinary attention.

This is where I separate support from treatment. Supporting normal digestion and immune function may be useful for overall health, but an active ear or skin infection needs the right diagnosis and care. Parasites likewise require testing and targeted treatment; no probiotic chew should be presented as a dewormer.

What I tell dog parents

Look for the pattern, then build the routine.

Start with consistent meals, appropriate exercise, fresh water, fewer random treats, and a daily record of stool, appetite, licking and comfort. If something is severe or keeps returning, bring that record to your vet. It can shorten the path from “something feels off” to a useful plan.

The 30-day gut-support routine I recommend

Thirty days is a practical observation window, not a guaranteed cure or countdown. The goal is to remove guesswork, stay consistent, and judge the whole pattern rather than one good or bad day.

Days 1–7Keep food and treats steady. Begin the gut-support routine as directed and record stool quality, gas, appetite and comfort each day.
Days 8–21Stay consistent. Avoid changing several variables at once. Note trends in grass eating, paw licking and digestive comfort without assuming every change came from one product.
Day 30Review your notes. If your dog is doing well, decide whether the routine is worth continuing. If signs persist, worsen or concern you, share the record with your veterinarian.
